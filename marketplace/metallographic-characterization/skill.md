# Metallographic Characterization

Prepare and examine metallographic cross-sections to characterize the microstructure, hardness profile, and any degradation mechanisms in the bulk material.

## Inputs

- `fracture-mechanism`: Identified fracture mechanism guiding where to section
- `failed-component`: Material available for destructive cross-sectioning (away from the fracture surface)

## Outputs

- `microstructure-report`: Metallographic images showing grain structure, phase distribution, inclusions, and any anomalies (decarburization, carburization, sensitization, hydrogen damage)
- `hardness-profile`: Micro-hardness traverses across relevant features (surface to core, HAZ, case depth) with comparison to specification requirements

## Process

1. Select sectioning locations to answer specific questions. Section through the fracture origin to see the crack profile and any subsurface features. Section through an unfailed area to establish the baseline microstructure. For welded components, section through the weld to characterize the weld metal, fusion line, HAZ, and base metal. Section perpendicular to any secondary cracks.
2. Mount, grind, and polish per standard metallographic practice (ASTM E3). Final polish with 1 or 0.05 micron alumina or colloidal silica. Etch with the appropriate reagent: 2% nital for carbon and low-alloy steels (reveals ferrite grain boundaries, pearlite, bainite, martensite). Vilella's reagent for stainless steels (reveals prior austenite grain boundaries and sensitization). Kroll's reagent for titanium (reveals alpha and beta phases).
3. Examine the microstructure. Compare to the material specification requirements. Look for: decarburization at the surface (loss of carbon = soft surface = fatigue initiation), abnormal grain growth (loss of toughness), sensitization in stainless steel (chromium carbide precipitation at grain boundaries = intergranular corrosion path), hydrogen damage (blistering, flaking, decarburization), improper heat treatment (wrong phase distribution, inadequate tempering).
4. Perform micro-hardness testing (Vickers HV0.5 or HV1) across the features of interest. For case-hardened parts, traverse from surface to core (effective case depth is defined at a specific hardness, e.g., 550 HV). For welds, traverse across weld metal, HAZ, and base metal (HAZ softening in quenched and tempered steels, HAZ hardening in as-rolled steels). Hardness above 350 HV in the HAZ of carbon steel indicates a susceptibility to hydrogen cracking.
