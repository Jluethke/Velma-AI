# Third Order And Feedback Loops

Map third-order consequences for the most important second-order effects, and identify any feedback loops that could amplify or dampen over time.

## Inputs

- `second-order-consequences`: Second-order effects
- `second-order-surprises`: Counterintuitive effects

## Outputs

- `third-order-effects`: For the most important second-order consequences: what happens after that? (Only map 2-3 third-order chains, not everything)
- `feedback-loops`: Any self-reinforcing or self-dampening loops identified across the three orders of consequences

## Process

1. Select the 2-3 most important second-order consequences and ask 'and then what?' one more time. Third-order consequences are speculative, so only invest the analysis in the most impactful chains.
2. Look for feedback loops: a third-order consequence that feeds back into a first-order cause. Example: higher prices lead to fewer but better customers (first/second), who generate better case studies (third), which attract more high-quality customers (feeds back to first). This is a positive feedback loop.
3. Also look for negative feedback loops: consequences that dampen themselves over time. These are self-correcting and less dangerous.
4. The feedback loops are the most important output of the entire analysis. A decision that triggers a positive feedback loop is much more valuable than one with only linear effects. A decision that triggers a negative feedback loop may undo itself.
