# Second Order Consequence Map

For each first-order consequence, ask 'and then what?' to map the second-order consequences.

## Inputs

- `first-order-consequences`: Immediate consequences of each option
- `first-order-confidence`: Confidence levels

## Outputs

- `second-order-consequences`: For each first-order consequence: 2-3 consequences of THAT consequence (what happens because the first-order effect happened)
- `second-order-surprises`: Second-order consequences that are counterintuitive or opposite to what the first-order effect would suggest

## Process

1. Take each first-order consequence and ask: 'And then what happens because of this?' If raising the price causes some customers to leave (first order), what happens next? The remaining customers may be higher-quality (second order). Competitors may interpret the price increase as confidence (second order).
2. Look specifically for second-order consequences that contradict the first-order effect. A price increase loses some customers (bad, first order) but increases perceived value for the remaining customers (good, second order).
3. This is where most analysis stops and where the best analysis begins. Second-order thinking reveals effects that are invisible from the first-order perspective.
4. Flag any second-order consequence that could become a feedback loop: a consequence that amplifies itself over time.
