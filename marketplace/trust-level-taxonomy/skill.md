# Trust Level Taxonomy

Define the levels of trust and the observable evidence for each, so you can accurately assess where any relationship stands.

## Inputs

- `relationship-type`: The context: personal, professional, sales, partnership, or general purpose
- `risk-tolerance`: Your tolerance for relationship risk (conservative: slow escalation, aggressive: faster escalation)

## Outputs

- `trust-levels`: Defined levels from zero-trust to full-trust with behavioral markers for each
- `evidence-checklist`: For each level, the specific observable behaviors that confirm the other party has reached that trust level

## Process

1. Define 5 trust levels. Level 0 (transactional): you exchange only what is required for a specific purpose. Level 1 (informational): you share non-sensitive insights, opinions on public topics, and general advice. Level 2 (reputational): you stake your reputation on each other (make introductions, give public endorsements, co-create content). Level 3 (strategic): you share non-public information about plans, opportunities, and vulnerabilities. Level 4 (committed): you make significant commitments (invest money, dedicate time, take career risks on each other's behalf).
2. For each level, define the evidence that someone is AT that level, not just what YOU are willing to do. Level 2 is not 'I would introduce them to my contacts' but 'they introduced me to their contacts without being asked.' Trust is verified by what the other party does when they have the option not to.
3. Define the asymmetry rule: trust levels can be different in each direction. You may trust someone at level 3 while they trust you at level 1. Asymmetric trust is common and not necessarily a problem, but extreme asymmetry (you at level 4, them at level 1) is a vulnerability.
4. Map common trust illusions: interactions that feel like trust but are not. Someone who shares gossip with everyone is not at level 3 with you specifically. Someone who makes introductions as a networking tactic is at level 2 for instrumental reasons, not relational ones. The evidence checklist must distinguish genuine trust from habitual behavior.
